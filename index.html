Task 1

I implemented one step of de Casteljau evaluation by linearly interpolating each adjacent pair of control points. For each evaluation, I keep the same parameter \(t \in [0,1]\) at every level. I used the following interpolation rule:

\[\mathbf{b}_i^{k+1} = (1-t)\,\mathbf{b}_i^k + t\,\mathbf{b}_{i+1}^k\]

In evaluateStep , I loop through adjacent pairs, calculate their interpolated positions, and append each result to a new vector. I return this vector so the viewer can repeat the operation.

To demo, I created a curve with six control points in bzc/curve6.bzc . I used (0.12, 0.24), (0.25, 0.82), (0.48, 0.10), (0.70, 0.90), (0.94, 0.25), and (1.16, 0.62). I captured every evaluation level at \(t = 0.5\), with the complete curve shown in green. The wider-than-square viewer permits the last x coordinate to exceed 1.

I then moved the third control point from (0.48, 0.10) to (0.48, 0.32) and the fourth from (0.70, 0.90) to (0.70, 0.75). I used the scroll control to change \(t\) from \(0.5\) to \(0.7\). I observed that moving the control points changed the shape of the curve, while changing \(t\) moved the evaluated point along it.

Task 2

I extended de Casteljau evaluation to Bezier surfaces by applying the same interpolation to 3D control points in two stages. I first evaluate each row of the control-point grid at \(u\). I then use those resulting points as the control points of the moving Bezier curve shown in lecture and evaluate it at \(v\). This gives one point on the surface at \((u,v)\):

\[\begin{aligned} \mathbf{q}_i(u) &= \operatorname{deCasteljau}(\mathbf{P}_{i0},\ldots,\mathbf{P}_{i,n-1};u),\\ \mathbf{P}(u,v) &= \operatorname{deCasteljau}(\mathbf{q}_0(u),\ldots,\mathbf{q}_{n-1}(u);v). \end{aligned}\]

In evaluateStep, I interpolate adjacent Vector3D points using the supplied parameter. In evaluate1D, I copy the input points and repeat this step until only one point remains. In evaluate, I call evaluate1D on every row with \(u\), collect the results in a vector, and call it once more on that vector with \(v\). I keep the original control points unchanged.

Task 3

I calculate a vertex normal by combining the normals of all triangles incident to that vertex, weighted by their areas. I start from the vertex's outgoing halfedge and use twin()->next() to move around the vertex. For each triangle, I take the cross product of the two edge vectors that start at the vertex:

\[\mathbf{c}_f=(\mathbf{p}_1-\mathbf{p}_0)\times(\mathbf{p}_2-\mathbf{p}_0),\qquad \mathbf{n}_v=\frac{\sum_{f\ni v}\mathbf{c}_f}{\left\|\sum_{f\ni v}\mathbf{c}_f\right\|}.\]

I leave each cross product unnormalized before adding it to the sum. Its magnitude is twice the triangle's area, so larger triangles contribute more to the final direction. I normalize the sum once at the end. I use HalfedgeCIter because Vertex::normal() is a const function, and I skip virtual boundary faces. If the sum is zero, I return a zero vector.

I compared dae/teapot.dae with flat shading and with area-weighted vertex normals. With flat shading, I can see the individual triangles across the body and lid. With vertex normals, the lighting changes smoothly across those surfaces. I used Q to toggle between the two shading modes.

Task 4

I implemented an edge flip by replacing the shared diagonal of two adjacent triangles with the diagonal between their opposite vertices. In my code, I label the original endpoints \(a,b\) and the opposite vertices \(c,d\). The two triangles change as follows:

\[(a,b,c),\ (b,a,d)\quad\longrightarrow\quad(c,d,b),\ (d,c,a).\]

I first save the six halfedges, four vertices, and two faces around the edge. I reuse the two halfedges of the original diagonal for the new edge \((c,d)\), then use setNeighbors to assign the next, twin, vertex, edge, and face pointers of all six halfedges. I also update the representative halfedges of the four vertices, the flipped edge, and the two faces. The surrounding edges keep their existing twins and edge objects.

Then, I return the original edge immediately if it is a boundary edge. For an interior edge, I only reassign pointers in this local neighborhood, so the operation takes constant time and does not create or delete vertices, edges, faces, or halfedges. I return the same edge iterator, which now represents the new diagonal.

To check my pointer assignments, I verified that each face forms a three-halfedge cycle, twins point back to each other, and traversing a vertex's outgoing halfedges visits all of its incident edges. I flipped the same edge 100 times, checked that boundary flips leave the pointers unchanged, and tested 300 flips on the teapot while checking connectivity, element counts, and vertex positions after each flip

Task 5

I implemented an edge split by inserting a vertex at the midpoint of the selected edge and connecting it to the opposite vertex of each neighboring triangle. Using the same labels as my edge flip, I split the edge \((a,b)\) and place the new vertex at

\[\mathbf{m}=\frac{\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b}}{2}.\]

For an interior edge, the two triangles become four: \((a,m,c)\), \((m,b,c)\), \((m,a,d)\), and \((m,d,b)\). I save the original neighborhood before changing its links, reuse the original edge for one half of the split edge, and add one vertex, three edges, two faces, and six halfedges. I assign the next, twin, vertex, edge, and face pointers with setNeighbors, then update the representative halfedges. I return the new vertex with its halfedge pointing along one half of the original edge. This uses a fixed number of local operations, so the split takes constant time.

I mark the midpoint vertex as new and the two edges connecting it to the opposite vertices as new. I mark both halves of the original edge as old so I can distinguish these edges when implementing subdivision. To check the pointer assignments, I verified the four resulting triangles, the exact element counts, the midpoint position, and the vertex and face traversal cycles. I also tested nearby splits and flips together: 60 splits and 91 flips on the cube, and 60 splits and 120 flips on the teapot, with connectivity checks after each operation.

I then flipped eight of the new interior edges in the split mesh. Compared with the original mesh above, the result has the added midpoint vertices and a different arrangement of diagonals. The surface stays connected through both operations.

Task 6

I implemented Loop subdivision using the weights and split-then-flip sequence. I first calculate all updated positions from the original mesh. For an interior vertex with \(n\) neighbors, I use

\[\mathbf{p}'=(1-nu)\mathbf{p}+u\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{p}_i,\qquad u=\begin{cases}\frac{3}{16},&n=3,\\\frac{3}{8n},&n\ne3.\end{cases}\]

For an interior edge with endpoints \(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\) and opposite vertices \(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{d}\), I calculate the inserted vertex as

\[\mathbf{m}'=\frac{3}{8}(\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b})+\frac{1}{8}(\mathbf{c}+\mathbf{d}).\]

I store the updated old-vertex positions in Vertex::newPosition and the edge-point positions in Edge::newPosition. I keep position unchanged while calculating these values. I save the original edges in a vector so I split each one exactly once, even as the mesh gains edges. I transfer each saved edge-point position to its new vertex, then flip only edges marked new that connect one old vertex and one new vertex. I keep both halves of each original edge marked old. Finally, I copy every vertex's newPosition into position.

I checked the old-vertex and edge-point weights independently on the cube, teapot, a degree-three tetrahedron, and an open square. I also checked halfedge twins, triangle cycles, vertex traversal, and the counts \(V'=V+E\), \(E'=2E+3F\), and \(F'=4F\) after repeated subdivision.

I can see the teapot's triangles become smaller and its silhouette become smoother after each subdivision. On the cube, the sharp corners and edges round off and the surface shrinks toward a smooth shape.

I observed that the original cube becomes asymmetric. Each square face has a diagonal, and those diagonals give the corners different neighbor sets and degrees. The old-vertex weights depend on those degrees, and the new-edge weights depend on the opposite vertices. I think this is because the subdivision doesn't maintain the same symetry as the geometric cube.

I made the cube subdivide symmetrically by splitting the diagonal on each of its six square faces. This puts a vertex at each face center and replaces each pair of triangles with a four-triangle fan. The preprocessed cube has 14 vertices and 24 triangles, and corresponding corners now have matching neighborhoods under the cube's rotations.

I also tried reducing the loss of sharp features by subdividing every triangle once using midpoint splits and the same flips, without moving the old vertices. This adds vertices along the original edges before applying the smoothing weights. After two Loop iterations, I see broader flat regions and less shrinkage than in the cube subdivided directly.

Task 6 Extra Credit: Boundary Meshes

I implemented subdivision for meshes with boundaries inside MeshResampler::upsample. For a boundary vertex, I use only its two neighbors along the boundary. For a boundary edge, I insert its midpoint. I use the following boundary rules, also described in Physically Based Rendering:

\[\mathbf{p}'=\frac{3}{4}\mathbf{p}+\frac{1}{8}(\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{left}}+\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{right}}),\qquad \mathbf{m}'=\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b}).\]

I use a boundary-splitting helper within Task 6 to split the one real triangle adjacent to a boundary edge and insert the new halfedge into the virtual boundary loop. I update the representative halfedges and mark the two halves of the original boundary edge as old.

I tested an open square made of two triangles. It has eight triangles after one subdivision and 32 after two. I also tested three successive subdivisions of a single open triangle and checked both boundary weights and connectivity.
